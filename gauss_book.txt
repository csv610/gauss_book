                                    Preface

Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855), often called the “Prince of Mathematicians,” left an in-
delible mark on virtually every branch of mathematics. From his teenage discovery of the
construction of the regular heptadecagon to his monumental Disquisitiones Arithmeticae,
from the method of least squares to the fundamental theorem of algebra, from Gaussian
elimination to the Gaussian distribution—his contributions form the bedrock of modern
mathematics.
    This book takes a computational approach to exploring Gauss’s mathematics. Rather
than merely presenting theorems and proofs, we implement Gauss’s algorithms, reproduce
his calculations, and extend his ideas using Python. Each chapter focuses on a major area
of Gauss’s work, providing:

   • Historical context and mathematical background

   • Rigorous mathematical development with proofs

   • Python implementations of Gauss’s algorithms

   • Numerical experiments and visualizations

   • Exercises for deeper exploration

    Who this book is for: Students of mathematics, computer science, and physics; re-
searchers interested in computational number theory, numerical analysis, and mathematical
history; and anyone who wants to experience Gauss’s mathematics through the lens of mod-
ern computation.
    Prerequisites: Basic knowledge of Python programming, linear algebra, calculus, and
elementary number theory. Familiarity with numpy, scipy, and matplotlib is helpful but
not required—we introduce these tools as needed.
    Structure of the book:

   • Part I: Number Theory (Chapters 1–4) covers modular arithmetic, quadratic reci-
     procity, Gauss sums, and the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae.

   • Part II: Analysis and Special Functions (Chapters 5–7) explores the Gaussian
     integral, theta functions, and the arithmetic-geometric mean.

   • Part III: Linear Algebra and Numerical Methods (Chapters 8–10) covers Gaus-
     sian elimination, least squares, and numerical integration.

                                            iii
Preface                                                                             iv


   • Part IV: Statistics and Probability (Chapters 11–12) examines the normal distri-
     bution, error analysis, and Gaussian processes.

   • Part V: Geometry and Astronomy (Chapters 13–15) investigates the heptadecagon,
     differential geometry, and orbital mechanics.

   Each chapter includes a companion Python module in the python/ directory, with thor-
ough documentation and tests in the tests/ directory. A separate Student User Guide
provides step-by-step tutorials for running the code, modifying experiments, and extending
the implementations.
   Acknowledgments: We are grateful for the digitized works of Gauss available through
the Göttingen Digital Library, the comprehensive commentary in [gauss-works], and the
modern expositions in [edwards-fermat, stillwell-number-theory, cohen-number-theory].

Modular Arithmetic and Congruences

1.1        Introduction
Carl Friedrich Gauss revolutionized number theory with his seminal work Disquisitiones
Arithmeticae (1801), where he systematized the theory of congruences. The notion of modu-
lar arithmetic—working with remainders upon division—provides the foundation for modern
cryptography, coding theory, and computational number theory.
    In this chapter, we explore:

    • Congruences and residue classes

    • Modular arithmetic operations

    • The Chinese Remainder Theorem (Gauss’s proof)

    • Primitive roots and discrete logarithms

    • Gauss’s algorithm for computing discrete logarithms


1.2        Congruences and Residue Classes
Definition 1.1 (Congruence). Let a, b, n ∈ Z with n > 0. We say a is congruent to b modulo
n, written
                                    a ≡ b (mod n),
if n | (a − b), i.e., a − b = kn for some k ∈ Z.

    The relation ≡ (mod n) is an equivalence relation on Z. The equivalence classes are
called residue classes modulo n. The set of all residue classes is denoted Z/nZ or Zn .

Example 1.2. Modulo 7, the residue classes are:

[0]7 = {. . . , −7, 0, 7, 14, . . .},   [1]7 = {. . . , −6, 1, 8, 15, . . .},   ...,   [6]7 = {. . . , −1, 6, 13, 20, . . .}.

We typically represent each class by its least non-negative representative {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

Theorem 1.3 (Properties of Congruences). If a ≡ b (mod n) and c ≡ d (mod n), then:
  1. a + c ≡ b + d (mod n)
  2. a − c ≡ b − d (mod n)
  3. ac ≡ bd (mod n)
  4. ak ≡ bk (mod n) for any k ∈ N

1.4      Primitive Roots
Definition 1.5 (Primitive Root). Let p be an odd prime. An integer g is a primitive root
modulo p if the order of g modulo p is p − 1, i.e., the smallest positive integer k such that
g k ≡ 1 (mod p) is k = p − 1.
   Gauss proved that primitive roots exist for all prime moduli (Article 55). More generally,
primitive roots exist modulo n iff n = 2, 4, pk , 2pk for an odd prime p.
Algorithm 1.6 (Gauss’s Algorithm for Discrete Logarithm). Given a primitive root g mod-
ulo p and a ̸≡ 0 (mod p), find x such that g x ≡ a (mod p).
    Gauss’s method (Article 81) uses the fact that if g is a primitive root, then every non-zero
residue can be written uniquely as g x . The algorithm reduces the problem by working with
indices.

1.8     Historical Notes
Gauss’s Disquisitiones Arithmeticae was written when he was just 21 years old (published
at 24). The work introduced the notation a ≡ b (mod n) and systematically developed
the theory of congruences. Article 36–44 contains the Chinese Remainder Theorem with
Gauss’s own proof. Articles 52–60 establish the existence of primitive roots for prime moduli.
The discrete logarithm (index) calculus in Articles 81–85 was used by Gauss for extensive
numerical computations.

2.1     Introduction

provides a deep and unexpected connection between the solvability of x2 ≡ p (mod q) and

   • Algorithms for computing square roots modulo primes (Tonelli-Shanks, Cipolla)

2.12      Historical Notes
Gauss discovered quadratic reciprocity at age 18 (1796) and called it the “fundamental
theorem.” He published six proofs in his lifetime (1801, 1808, 1811, 1817, 1818, 1824) and
left two more in his unpublished papers. The eighth proof (using Gauss sums) appeared in
1818 and connects quadratic reciprocity to the theory of cyclotomic fields and the discrete
Fourier transform—a profound link between number theory and analysis that we explore in
Chapter 3.
    Gauss’s Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (Article 131) states: “The fundamental theorem of
quadratic reciprocity is one of the most elegant of its type... It has been the subject of many
investigations by the most celebrated geometers.”

          The Method of Least Squares

9.1     Introduction
The method of least squares is one of Gauss’s most influential contributions to science.
He developed it for analyzing astronomical observations (especially the orbit of Ceres) and
published it in Theoria Motus Corporum Coelestium (1809). The method minimizes the sum
of squared residuals and provides the best linear unbiased estimator under the Gauss-Markov
theorem.
    In this chapter, we cover:

   • Linear least squares: Ax ≈ b

   • Normal equations: AT Ax = AT b

   • QR decomposition solution

   • Weighted least squares

   • Regularization (Tikhonov, ridge regression)

   • Nonlinear least squares: Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt

   • Gauss’s application to the orbit of Ceres


9.2     Linear Least Squares
Given A ∈ Rm×n (m ≥ n) and b ∈ Rm , find x minimizing ∥Ax − b∥22 .

Theorem 9.1 (Normal Equations). The minimizer satisfies AT Ax = AT b. If A has full
column rank, AT A is invertible and x = (AT A)−1 AT b.

9.3. QR Solution                                                                        38


9.3     QR Solution
Using A = QR with QT Q = I, R upper triangular:

                         ∥Ax − b∥2 = ∥QRx − b∥2 = ∥Rx − QT b∥2 .

Solve Rx = QT b by back substitution. This is more stable than normal equations.


9.4     Weighted Least Squares
Minimize ∥W 1/2 (Ax − b)∥22 for symmetric positive definite weight matrix W . Solution:
(AT W A)x = AT W b.


9.5     Tikhonov Regularization
For ill-conditioned problems, minimize ∥Ax − b∥22 + λ∥x∥22 :

                                    (AT A + λI)x = AT b.


9.6     Nonlinear Least Squares
Minimize ∥f (x)∥22 where f : Rn → Rm . The Gauss-Newton method linearizes:

                               xk+1 = xk − (JkT Jk )−1 JkT f (xk )

where Jk is the Jacobian of f at xk .
  The Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm adds damping:

                            xk+1 = xk − (JkT Jk + λI)−1 JkT f (xk ).


9.7     Gauss and the Orbit of Ceres
In 1801, asteroid Ceres was discovered but lost. Gauss used least squares on three obser-
vations to predict its orbit, enabling its rediscovery. He solved a nonlinear least squares
problem for the six orbital elements.

12.1       Introduction
Gaussian processes (GPs) provide a principled, non-parametric approach to regression and
classification. They define a distribution over functions, with the covariance structure encod-
ing assumptions about smoothness, periodicity, and other properties. Kriging (geostatistics)
is the application of GPs to spatial interpolation, named after D.G. Krige and formalized by
Matheron.
    In this chapter, we cover:

   • Definition and properties of Gaussian processes

   • Covariance functions (kernels)

   • Gaussian process regression (Kriging)

   • Hyperparameter optimization

   • Scalable approximations for large datasets

   • Applications to spatial statistics and time series


12.2       Gaussian Processes
Definition 12.1 (Gaussian Process). A Gaussian process is a collection of random variables
{f (x) : x ∈ X } such that any finite subset (f (x1 ), . . . , f (xn )) has a multivariate normal
distribution.

   A GP is completely specified by its mean function m(x) = E[f (x)] and covariance function
k(x, x′ ) = Cov(f (x), f (x′ )):

14.1      Introduction
Gauss’s Theorema Egregium (1827) states that the Gaussian curvature of a surface is an
intrinsic invariant: it can be determined entirely from measurements within the surface (the
first fundamental form), without reference to the ambient space. This was a revolutionary
discovery that founded differential geometry.

   • First and second fundamental forms
   • Gaussian curvature and mean curvature
   • The Theorema Egregium
   • Geodesics and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem
   • Numerical computation of curvature

B.3      Linear Algebra
B.3.1     Gaussian Elimination
Forward elimination with partial pivoting, then back substitution. Complexity: O(n3 ).

B.3.2     LU Decomposition
P A = LU where P is permutation, L unit lower triangular, U upper triangular.

B.3.3     Cholesky Decomposition
For symmetric positive definite A: A = LLT with L lower triangular.

B.3.4     QR Decomposition
A = QR with Q orthogonal, R upper triangular. Householder reflections: H = I − 2uuT .

B.3.5     Condition Number
κ(A) = ∥A∥∥A−1 ∥ for a given norm. For 2-norm: κ2 (A) = σmax /σmin .

B.3.6     Least Squares
Minimize ∥Ax − b∥22 . Normal equations: AT Ax = AT b. QR solution: Rx = QT b (more
stable).
